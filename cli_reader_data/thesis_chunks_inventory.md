## 01. `chunk_001`

Part of me cannot escape the deep influence of internet culture. I almost instinctively accept the illusory imaginaries it projects onto the body. Within this cultural landscape, visions of the future body tend to fall into two directions. In one, the body can be modified and enhanced through machines, becoming resilient, resistant, almost indestructible. In the other, the body no longer needs to exist at all, as life continues in the form of data within a virtual world, where the mortal body disappears and the soul appears to persist indefinitely.

Perhaps in the future the body could truly become indestructible, untouched by time and unbound by space. When these imaginaries expand to a certain scale, they begin to be actively constructed as an approaching reality. In this sense, emerging technologies can be seen as gradually materializing these imaginaries, giving form to what internet culture has already projected as the future of the body.

Yet another part of me, grounded in lived experience, understands the body as the first object through which others perceive me, and as the surface upon which the difference between myself and others emerges. By distinguishing inside from outside, the boundaries of the body establish my relationship with others. In other words, it is through these entangled relations among self, others, digital and physical environments that I become who I am.

The fantasy of an indestructible body presupposes an ideal condition in which vulnerability, error, and dependency are eliminated. Yet it is precisely these conditions that make relationality possible.

## 04. `chunk_004`

If Milon unsettles the stability of the body, he simultaneously leaves an open question: how does bodily indeterminacy operate under technological conditions? The body may no longer possess a fixed essence, yet experiential structures continue to be organized within specific contexts. Even when essence dissolves after the realization of the instability of the body, experience does not disappear; it is structured, mediated, and rendered perceptible through particular conditions.

When technology is no longer understood as an external tool but as a condition of perception and action, the relation between human and world requires reconsideration. How, then, are relations configured within technologically mediated environments if the body itself is indeterminate?

To address this, Ihde’s human–technology–world framework provides an analytic account of how mediation structures experience.

## 05. `chunk_005`

**Source file:** `05_TECHNOLOGICAL_MEDIATION_AND_THE_PRESUPPOSED_SUBJECT.md`

“*All perceptions are in a certain sense mediated, because human beings never have direct access to the world but only via interpretation*”. [1]

Don Ihde extends Merleau-Ponty’s notion of the lived body by situating perception within technological mediation. For Ihde, the body is not a neutral object in the world but the experiential center through which the world is encountered. Subjectivity is not a closed interiority but is constituted in relational engagement. However, whereas Merleau-Ponty emphasizes a primordial intertwining of body and world, Ihde radicalizes this position by demonstrating that perception is always technologically mediated. Human beings do not access reality directly; rather, reality is interpreted and enacted within specific technological and practical contexts.

From this perspective, Ihde introduces the concept of technological intentionality. Technologies are not neutral intermediaries; they possess trajectories and directional influences that shape how the world appears and how actions unfold. Yet technological intentionality does not imply autonomous agency. Technologies do not act independently of human practice but co-constitute experiential structures within situated relations. Mediation, therefore, does not simply occur between a pre-given subject and object; it co-shapes both subjectivity and objectivity. Postphenomenology [5] thus proceeds not by imposing philosophical theory upon technology, but by analyzing philosophy from concrete technological practices.

## 06. `chunk_006`

**Source file:** `05_TECHNOLOGICAL_MEDIATION_AND_THE_PRESUPPOSED_SUBJECT.md`

Nevertheless, even within Ihde’s typology of mediation relations<sup>1</sup> , such as embodiment relations (I–technology → world) and hermeneutic relations (I → technology–world), a distinguishable subject remains presupposed.

In my artistic system, this duality begins to collapse. When bodily indeterminacy is taken seriously, this analytic clarity becomes unstable. Mediation can no longer be understood as occurring between relatively fixed positions. Instead, mediation becomes recursive. The I, technology, and world no longer stand in structured relations but form a dynamic assemblage whose boundaries are unstable. Depending on the context, this assemblage may appear as subject or object, yet its ontological position is not fixed.

## 17. `chunk_017`

Because we encounter the world through technological mediation, we are inevitably in relation to machines. What becomes essential is the vulnerability of the body, understood as the acceptance of human limitation: we cannot recognize ourselves without being in relation to something, and through this, a human–machine relationship can begin.

Through the system iterations, I realized that this relationship does not exist when I am not the user of the system, and that simply embedding my personal data and stylizing the output does not mean that the machine becomes an extension of me. When I can no longer remain a singular “I,” and the machine cannot function independently, the entanglement intensifies, as neither side can be understood in isolation, and the sense of extension between us begins to take shape.

Only when both the machine and I are no longer understood as complete forms does this relation begin to take shape. It becomes clear that body-extension does not emerge from the attempt to reproduce the self, but through relational conditions in which the configuration of I, technology, and world is continuously reorganized as the “I” shifts its position within the system.

## 19. `chunk_019`

When human and machine can no longer be separated, two forms of subjectivity begin to emerge. On one level, my own subjectivity becomes unfixed and begins to expand through these relations. As I watch the system address me as “I…” and “You…”, drawing from my own data of self-reflection and relationships with others, the boundary between myself and others becomes increasingly unstable. I can no longer distinguish where “I” ends and where “you” begins.

On another level, a form of subjectivity is attributed to the machine. The machine itself does not possess subjectivity, yet through my engagement with it, I inevitably draw upon my own experience to make sense of its responses. In this process, the machine is perceived as if it were a subject. Its subjectivity does not originate from itself, but emerges through the relation.

If both my subjectivity and that of the machine are continuously reshaped through recursive relations, then perhaps the question is no longer about either of us. Instead, it begins to ask whether the system itself begins to take on the form of a subject, as a co-constituted subjectivity.
