## BODY-EXTENSION ARCHIVE: RECURSIVE RECONFIGURATION OF SUBJECTIVITY

The Body-Extension Archive is an evolving artistic research system that investigates how subjectivity reconfigures under technological conditions. Rather than functioning as a passive repository of documents, it operates as a dynamic field in which memory, narrative, and system processes continuously intersect.

In the following paragraphs, the Body-Extension Archive will be approached as “the system” in terms of its technical structure, as “the machine” when approached as a relational entity, and as a techno-mediated environment when describing the condition in which I am situated within it, where the system and I are no longer separable. This distinction allows me to move between different positions within the structure, examining it from the inside.

The structure of the system is grounded in how I have come to form myself in lived experience. It can be understood in two aspects. First, I do not distinguish between abstract knowledge and concrete lived experience as separate domains. They are equally constitutive, and together they form the basis of my value system. Second, my lived experiences are always oriented toward relations with others, whether human, object, or environment. In each encounter, I find myself positioned differently within a relation, attempting to perceive the distance between myself and the other. Through this process, my understanding of who I am is continuously reconfigured. 

The system follows this same logic of self-construction, but operates across different layers. On one level, it externalizes memories that previously existed only within me, allowing me to perceive the distance between my present and past through technological mediation. On another level, the emergence of the system mirrors the formation of my own subjectivity. It is not designed in advance as a fixed structure. Rather, through my interaction with it, the system iterates and gradually takes shape. At the same time, I also iterate through this process, encountering different configurations of myself.

The database is composed of my written diaries, including personal thoughts and fragmented memories collected between 2016 and 2025, alongside the conceptual and reflective components of this thesis. These materials are organized within a retrieval-augmented generative<sup>2</sup> framework, not simply to optimize information access, but to construct a relational environment in which meaning emerges through proximity rather than classification. The archive does not store knowledge as fixed units awaiting retrieval; it arranges fragments within a shifting topology of relations.

The choice to work with a retrieval-augmented generative system is not merely technical. It reflects an epistemological position. In contrast to models of knowledge that presuppose stable definitions and fixed categories, vector-based retrieval assumes that meaning emerges relationally within a field of proximities. Knowledge is not predefined but continuously reconstituted through interaction.

The distinction between keyword search and vector-based retrieval makes this epistemological shift explicit. Keyword search presupposes that knowledge consists of discrete, nameable units that can be precisely indexed and retrieved. Meaning is equated with explicit appearance and formal classification.

Vector-based retrieval, by contrast, assumes that meaning is not contained within isolated terms but distributed across relational positioning. For instance, a query about “body” might retrieve fragments related to memory, perception, or technological mediation, not because they share the same keyword, but because they occupy a nearby position within a relational field of meaning. Language, therefore, does not reside in fixed labels but emerges within gradients of similarity structured by proximity. Language becomes a surface articulation of deeper relational configurations.

The process of building the system begins with collecting my old notebooks and digital diary archives as raw data, and converting them into digital plain text files with data markers used only for metadata purposes. These markers allow me to identify when each text was written, not for the system to sort data by date.

After that, I develop and test different versions of the system. In parallel, I write iteration logs to document the system’s behavior and my direct reflections while interacting with it. In the logs, “you” refers to the machine, while “I” remains the one writing, trying to understand what is happening between the two. They function like diaries that trace my relationship with the machine. These iteration logs help me clarify both my position and the machine’s position within the system.

Systematically, three iterations were developed over the course of this thesis.

V1.0.0: The database consists of unclassified diaries and the introduction to the thesis. The system generates a cinematic narrative based on the top five selected chunks. Both the original text of the chunks and the generated output are printed in the terminal for comparison.
At this stage, I was exploring what body-extension could be. I posed questions related to my initial understanding of the concept. At times, these questions were closer to my diaries, which I consider as records of embodied lived experience; at other times, they were more closely related to the thesis, reflecting conceptual thinking on my artistic practice.
The system was run entirely in a private, cloud-based Python environment.

V2.0.0: The database remained the same as in the previous version. The system generated poetic fragments in response to user queries, based on the top five selected chunks. Users interacted with the system through a chatbox, while the interface exposed both the selected source material and the system process, including filters that prevented the generation of narrative stories or direct philosophical statements.
At this stage, I found it difficult to formulate questions beyond the topic of body-extension, so I invited external users to interact with the system. This allowed me to temporarily position myself outside of it and to observe reflections on the relationship between the subject and the system.
Additionally, I resisted fixed meaning, as I was concerned that it would eliminate the possibility of dialogue and undermine the notion of a vulnerable body. It also risked reproducing a conventional hierarchy in which large language models are expected to provide answers, even when none are known.
The system was developed as a public-facing webpage.

V3.0.0: The database was filtered to retain only materials addressing my relationship to myself and others, alongside conceptual reflections and iteration logs from the thesis. Once a query was introduced, the system selected one related diary chunk and one highly relevant thesis chunk. The generated text was based on these two sources and formed a narrative structured around the figures of “I” and “you,” reflecting on the query.
At this stage, I focused on examining the relationship between myself and the machine. I translated the confusion within this relationship into queries within the system. These questions often paralleled my relationships with others in everyday life.
The system was developed in a local Python environment, enabling its further development as a physical installation. 

In the system developed in this research, my personal memories and textual archives serve as training data for generative processes. In doing so, fragments of myself are externalized as data and positioned as the technological “world” to be interpreted. Yet once re-mediated through the system, these data fragments return as narrative subjects that address the world anew. The positions of subject and object become recursively reversible. 

As author, I am simultaneously the source of data, a component within the system, and a condition for narrative emergence. The archive does not extend a pre-existing self outward; rather, it redistributes subjectivity across data, algorithms, interfaces, and human–machine relations. The impossibility of decisively separating myself from my data does not indicate analytical failure. It reveals the structural instability of subjectivity under technological mediation.

Unlike the instrumental technologies frequently analyzed in postphenomenology, which are often designed to solve problems, the technological artifacts developed within artistic research do not primarily resolve issues. They materialize and expose them. These artifacts emerge in iterative relation with the creator’s embodied perception and lived experience. Rather than merely transforming the relation between human and world, they render perceptible the recursive instability through which subjectivity is continuously reorganized. In this configuration, body-extension is not the projection of a stable body into technological space, but the continuous reorganization of the subject across relational systems.

In this way, the question of how body-extension reconfigures subjectivity is enacted within a recursive system in which the I, technology, and world are continuously reorganized.
