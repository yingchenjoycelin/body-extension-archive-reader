1.
Don Ihde systematizes the Human–Technology–World structure as a triadic framework for understanding mediated experience [1]. Rather than conceiving experience as a simple subject–object relation, he argues that human–world relations are always technologically mediated and can be differentiated into distinct relational structures.


The first is the embodiment relation, structured as (I–technology) → world. In this configuration, technology is incorporated into the user’s bodily schema and functions as an extension of perception. Technologies such as eyeglasses become experientially transparent, allowing the user to perceive the world through them rather than thematically attending to them.

The second is the hermeneutic relation, structured as I → (technology–world). Here, technology does not withdraw into bodily transparency but instead presents representations that require interpretation. Instruments such as thermometers or infrared imaging devices mediate the world symbolically; the user must read or interpret the technological output in order to understand the environment.

The third is the alterity relation, structured as I → technology (–world). In this case, technology itself becomes the focus of interaction and appears as a quasi-other. The user engages with the technology as if it were an entity in its own right, as in interactions with automated systems or conversational interfaces.

Through this typology, Ihde demonstrates that technologies do not mediate experience in a uniform manner; rather, they reconfigure the human–world relation according to distinct experiential structures.

The framework of a classic Large Language Model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, can be understood as a direct process: a query is entered, processed by the model, and a response is generated.

A “retrieval-augmented generation” (RAG) system introduces an additional step. Instead of relying only on pre-trained knowledge, it first retrieves relevant information from an external dataset.

To do this, both queries and stored data are converted into numerical representations called vectors. These vectors are arranged in a space where proximity corresponds to semantic similarity. This process is known as vector-based retrieval.
When a query is entered, the system retrieves the most relevant data based on this similarity and uses it to generate a response [3].
